1

Your body is a circuit

Every time you wave, kick, or blink, an electrical signal travels from your brain to a muscle. It works a lot like electricity lighting up a bulb.

2

Nerves are the wires

A nerve cell has a long arm called an axon. Many axons are wrapped in myelin, a fatty coating that works like the plastic around a wire. Myelin lets the signal jump from gap to gap, super fast.

Wow factSome nerve cells stretch from your spine to your toes. That's up to 1 meter long, in a single cell!
3

Muscles do the work

You have more than 600 muscles. They can only pull, never push, so they work in pairs. To bend your arm, the biceps squeezes. To straighten it, the triceps squeezes.

4

The neuromuscular junction

This is the spot where a nerve hands its message to a muscle. They don't actually touch. There's a tiny gap between them.

1 · Signal arrives

The electrical signal reaches the end of the nerve.

2 · Bubbles open

Tiny bubbles release a chemical messenger called acetylcholine (ACh).

3 · Key in a lock

ACh crosses the gap and fits into receptors on the muscle.

4 · Squeeze!

The muscle fiber contracts. The whole handoff takes about 1/1000 of a second.

5

When the circuit breaks

A neuromuscular disease happens when part of this circuit stops working well, so muscles get weak, tired, or hard to control. Doctors sort these diseases by where the break is, from the brain all the way down to the muscle. None of them are contagious.

The brain's balance center

Ataxia

From a Greek word meaning "without order"

Ataxia affects coordination, the smooth teamwork that keeps you steady when you walk, write, or talk. It often involves the cerebellum, the part of the brain that fine-tunes every move.

DiscoveredOne common type was first described in 1863 by German doctor Nikolaus Friedreich.
What helpsPhysical, occupational, and speech therapy help people stay steady and active. The first medicine for Friedreich's ataxia was approved in 2023.
Nerve cells in the brain and spinal cord

ALS

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis, also called Lou Gehrig's disease after a famous baseball player

In ALS, the motor neurons, the nerve cells that carry "move!" messages to muscles, gradually send fewer signals. With fewer messages, muscles have a harder time doing their job.

DiscoveredFirst described in 1869 by French doctor Jean-Martin Charcot.
What helpsMedicines can slow it down, and therapy and helpful tools keep people independent. Our lab is studying ALS to develop treatments.
The nerves

CMT

Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease, named after three doctors

CMT affects the nerves in the arms and legs, or their myelin coating, so signals travel more slowly. The longest nerves feel it first, so it usually shows up in the feet and lower legs.

DiscoveredFirst described in 1886 by Jean-Martin Charcot and Pierre Marie in France and Howard Henry Tooth in England.
What helpsAnkle braces, special shoes, and physical therapy keep people moving. Our lab is studying CMT to develop treatments.
The neuromuscular junction

Myasthenia gravis

From Greek and Latin words about muscle weakness

Here the nerve and the muscle are both healthy, but the handoff between them doesn't work as well, so the message has trouble crossing the gap. Muscles get tired with use and bounce back after rest.

DiscoveredFirst described in 1672 by English doctor Thomas Willis.
What helpsMedicines that help the message cross the gap work well, and most people live full, active lives. Our lab is studying myasthenia gravis to develop treatments.
The muscle

Muscular dystrophy

A group of muscle conditions

In muscular dystrophy, muscle fibers are missing a building-block protein, like dystrophin, that helps them stay strong. Over time the muscles have a harder time keeping up.

DiscoveredDescribed in the 1850s and 1860s by doctors Edward Meryon in England and Guillaume Duchenne in France.
What helpsPhysical therapy and medicines help. Our lab is studying muscular dystrophy to develop treatments.

What our lab does

At Neuro-Muscular Labs, scientists and doctors at the University of Missouri work together to find out exactly where the circuit breaks, and how to fix it.

Glowing microscopes

We light up nerves, myelin, and junctions in bright colors to see where things go wrong.

Measuring signals

We test how fast and how strong nerve and muscle signals are, in the lab and in patients.

Studying genes

We study the genes behind these diseases, using models like mice, to find new ways to help.

New treatments

We help test new medicines and gene therapies, moving discoveries from the lab bench to patients.
